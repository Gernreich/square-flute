Square-bore flute

Read the README

A five-hole minor-pentatonic flute in A4, in the Native American style, rebuilt in 3mm plywood with a square or rectangular bore. It keeps the bore area of a 7/8in (22.2mm) round-bore flute, 387mm², so the air column is the same in a section a laser can build. All closed it sounds A4; opening the holes from the foot up gives C5, D5, E5, G5 and A5.

Output is millimetre-true — 1 user unit = 1 mm with a physical width/height — so it prints and cuts at real size.

None of these builds has been cut or played yet. The holes and the foot come from an acoustic model, and the foot is cut long to be trimmed to pitch. The author's own flute of 2017, which has been, is here too, scaled to A4, and most of its ideas are in every build.

  • The box is four plates, finger-jointed edge to edge: the sides' fingers fill notches in the edges of the top and the bottom and run flush to the outside faces, so no plate is left outboard of a joint. The plug is four discs pressed into the bore.
  • The stack is a bottom layer, seven bore layers with the bore cut out of them and the plug left solid, and the top.
  • The 6mm builds glue a 3mm doubler over the run of finger holes, so each hole has a 6mm chimney, as a wooden flute's wall would give it.

Every build carries the author's mouthpiece cap from the 2017 flute below: a plate glued over the mouth end, with a half-round notch in its bottom edge, away from the finger holes. The notch is a 9.59mm circle centred 0.33mm inside the edge, 5.1mm deep, so it opens through the bottom wall into the slow air chamber.

Every build has the 2017 flute's window and exit hole too: both run the whole width of the bore, with the flue joining them across that same width. And every finger hole sits in the 2017 flute's soft engraved depression, its ring.png kept in the original's proportion to the hole, 1.57 times it across; on a 6mm build the depressions are on the doubler, where the finger lands. In the box no finger stands beside either opening, since a full-width opening reaches the inner face of the bore, which is the floor of every finger notch.

The box is square, the bore that was asked for. The stack cannot be: it is a whole number of 3mm layers deep, 18 or 21mm, and 19.67 is neither, so it keeps the area and gives up the square. There was a third bore, box-rect, the stack's rectangle built as a box to compare the two constructions at one section; they came out acoustically the same, so it went.

Every build is 484–487mm long, and all of them fit a 600 × 308mm bed.

Turn it in 3D

What the model says

The bore's shape barely matters. The square and the 18.43 × 21mm rectangle put every hole within 1.5mm of each other, and even that is the window rather than the bore: the window is as wide as the bore, so the narrower rectangle has the narrower window. At one window width the two agree to 0.1mm. Box or stack is a building choice, not an acoustic one.

The wall's thickness matters most. With the holes in 6mm of wall instead of 3mm, every one of them sits 11–17mm nearer the window, because a hole's chimney is part of its length.

The holes are laid out for the hands. In 3mm of wall, five equal 8mm holes land 28, 31, 47 and 15mm apart, and two fingers will not cover holes 15mm apart; in 6mm of wall five equal holes cannot reach all five notes at all. So the lower hand takes holes 1–3 and the upper hand 4–5, each hand's holes 25mm apart, and each hole is sized to its note. A bird-flue build and its nest-flue counterpart share a layout:

The scale as ratios

Each note's frequency against the lowest one, as the just ratio that names the interval, and the step up from the note below it:

Note Interval from A4 Ratio from A4 Step from the note below Just, cents Tuned to, cents
A4 unison 1:1 0 0
C5 minor third 6:5 6:5, a minor third 315.6 300
D5 perfect fourth 4:3 10:9, a minor whole tone 498.0 500
E5 perfect fifth 3:2 9:8, a major whole tone 702.0 700
G5 minor seventh 9:5 6:5, a minor third 1017.6 1000
A5 octave 2:1 10:9, a minor whole tone 1200.0 1200

The steps run 6:5, 10:9, 9:8, 6:5, 10:9: two minor thirds and three whole tones, and no semitone anywhere: no two notes are a half step apart. The model tunes to equal temperament, the last column, so every flute here is designed within 18 cents of the just ratios: the minor third and the minor seventh sit 15.6 and 17.6 cents flat of 6:5 and 9:5, and the fourth and the fifth are 2 cents off. The minor seventh has two other common just values: 16:9 at 996.1 cents and the harmonic 7:4 at 968.8.

Before you cut

The window's end correction is not measured. It is the least certain term in the model, and no built flute has checked it yet. Each 1mm of error in it moves the foot about 2.6mm. So the sound chamber is cut 15mm long: trim the foot until the flute sounds A4 with every hole closed.

The holes move with it, almost as far. A 10% error in that correction moves the foot 2.8mm and every hole 2.3–2.6mm. Trimming the foot puts the fundamental right but leaves the holes where the model put them, so expect to tune each one. A hole that plays flat can be opened with a file. A hole that plays sharp cannot be closed up.

7/8in is a large bore for A4. Flutopedia's bore table puts A4 nearer 3/4in. This is the bore that was asked for, but most flutes in this key are narrower.

The flue is a fill engrave, about 1mm deep and the full width of the bore. In the -bird-flue builds it is on the underside of the bottom bird layer, woodland style. In the -nest-flue builds and the 2017 flute, it is on the top plate over the plug, plains style. The depth is a power setting, so find it on scrap first.

File the splitting edge by hand. The window's far edge, where the air splits, needs a clean bevel of about 30°, and a laser cuts square, charred edges. File it before assembly, while the top is still a flat plate on the bench; if the edge goes wrong, recut the top.

Cut it in 3mm Baltic birch plywood, at a 0.13mm kerf. The finger joints are the ones on the trumpet bores in trumpet-elbows-allowed: Boxes.py's own, with 6mm fingers and 6mm spaces and no play, so the fit comes from the kerf compensation alone. The plug discs are drawn 0.12mm over the bore, the mallet fit used on the lyre-harp. Press-fit, then seal every seam with shellac. The doubler, the bird's layers, every layer of the stack and every mouthpiece cap are glued.

Run the colours blue, orange, black: blue engraves the part numbers and the flue, orange cuts the holes, slots and windows, and black frees the parts. That is the sequence every LaserMadeMusic repository uses. Every build also carries a greyscale image round each finger hole, the depression. It needs an image engrave that turns grey into depth, such as Grayscale mode in xTool Creative Space.

Tuning

  1. Tie the bird on over the exit hole, its front edge on the window's near edge. It is tied rather than glued, so it can be slid to voice the flute.
  2. With every hole closed, trim the foot until the flute sounds A4.
  3. Open hole 1 and tune C5. The holes are cut to the model's size, so if a note is flat, open its hole slightly with a file.
  4. Work up the flute in order, since each hole nudges the ones above it.

Regenerating it

python3 naf_model.py                          # the holes for every build, printed
python3 naf_model.py --mouth=0.8              # with a different window correction
python3 square_flute.py --no-write            # the generator's checks, writing nothing
python3 square_flute.py --out=cut-files       # the bird-flue builds
python3 plus_flute.py --out=cut-files         # the nest-flue builds
python3 your_flute.py --out=yours/cut-files   # the 2017 flute, at A4
python3 page_assets.py                        # viewer.js and the two plans

naf_model.py is a transmission-matrix model, the standard way woodwind calculators predict pitch. It treats the bore as a lossy tube, using the section's area and its hydraulic radius, which is where a square differs from a circle. Each finger hole is Keefe's tone hole, open or closed. At the top, the window opens into the bore beside a short closed stub up to the plug.

square_flute.py builds each bird-flue build from what the model asks for and checks it before writing anything:

  • every note within a cent of its target
  • every part on the bed
  • every hole inside the bore
  • enough ply between neighbouring holes, and between a hole and a finger notch
  • the fingers flush with the outside faces
  • the top in one piece
  • every part placed on a sheet

It borrows the stroke font for its part numbers from ribbon_bore.py in trumpet-elbows-allowed, so it expects that repository cloned beside this one.

Bird flue or nest flue: woodland and plains

Each build comes as a pair, named for the one thing that differs between them: where the flue is carved. In a -bird-flue build it is engraved in the underside of the bird. In a -nest-flue build it is engraved in the nest, the top plate's face over the plug, and the bird is three plain layers.

They are the two traditional places for a Native American flute's flue, and they are kept as a pair on purpose:

Where the flue is carved Style Here
-bird-flue the underside of the block, the bird woodland box-square-3-bird-flue … stack-rect-6-bird-flue
-nest-flue the flute's body, the nest under the bird plains, or prairie box-square-3-nest-flue … stack-rect-6-nest-flue, and the 2017 flute

The two put the airstream at different heights. A flue carved in the bird has the nest's flat surface as its floor, so the air leaves just above the top plate. A flue carved in the nest has the bird's flat underside as its roof, so the air leaves just below the top plate's surface. Against the splitting edge on the window's far side, that is a shift of about one flue depth, roughly 1mm, which is one reason the two styles voice differently. The model does not simulate the jet, so which suits these flutes is for a test cut to show.

A third traditional way forms the flue with a spacer plate: a thin plate with a slot cut through it, between a flat bird and a flat nest. Its floor is the nest, so the air leaves above the top plate exactly as from a bird flue; only the making differs. The depth is the spacer's thickness rather than an engrave, so it is exact and even; the floor and roof are clean ply faces rather than a charred groove; a different depth is a different spacer, not a new bird; but it is one more surface to seal, and it wants stock about 1mm thick, not 3mm. None of the builds here uses one. Flutopedia's anatomy of the flute and its glossary describe all three.

Where the flue is carved moves no hole, so the two of a pair share a hole layout, in the table above. The bird's layers and the cap are glued, as in the original. Two things are not taken. The engraved splitting edge charred, so the edge is still filed by hand. And the original's stepped block was for looks only, so the bird keeps its plain layers.

The author's flute

The builds above began as a fresh take, worked out without seeing this one. This flute was designed, cut and played in 2017, and its files are in yours/original/ exactly as they were. Measured out of yours/original/NAF_flatjoints.svg:

Bore 19.37 wide × 22.44mm deep, 435mm²
Sound chamber 313.25mm from the window's centre to the foot
Window 19.37 × 6.0mm, the whole width of the bore
Plug 11mm, its top engraved as the flue
Finger holes 7.73, 7.87, 7.79, 7.79 and 5.47mm, 213.9, 185.7, 156.9, 109.5 and 78.0mm from the window

It tried two things the first designs here did not:

  • An engraved splitting edge. FluteEdge.png is a greyscale ramp, black at the window and white 9.9mm on, engraved beside the window so the laser would cut the bevel itself. It did not work, so the edge is filed by hand, as it is in every build.
  • A soft engraved depression round each finger hole, ring.png, for the finger to find. These worked, cut on a Glowforge, whose 3D engrave reads a grey image as depth. On an xTool P2S, Grayscale mode in xTool Creative Space varies the power with each pixel's brightness, so the same image should work there too. Set its depth on scrap first.

Its flue is in the nest, an engrave over the plug, as in the -nest-flue builds.

Redrawn at A4

your_flute.py scales it to A4 and redraws it in two builds:

  • yours-flat-A4: the sides butted between the top and the bottom, and glued.
  • yours-fingers-A4: finger-jointed top and bottom, with the trumpet bores' joint.

The model hears the original near B♭4, as the last table below shows. To put it on A4, every dimension that sets the pitch is multiplied by one factor, 1.0705, solved so the model gives 440.00Hz: the bore, the window, each hole's diameter, depression and distance from the window, and the sound chamber. What ply fixes does not scale: the 3mm wall, the plug's four discs, the slow air chamber, the exit hole, the cap's notch and the stepped block.

Original At A4
Bore 19.37 × 22.44mm, 435mm² 20.74 × 24.02mm, 498mm²
Window 19.37 × 6.0mm 20.74 × 6.4mm
Sound chamber 313.3mm 335.3mm
Overall length 456.7mm 479.0mm

The hole diameters are re-solved rather than scaled. Scaling them too would leave the scale's shape behind: the wall stays 3mm while the holes grow, so each hole acts a little larger, and the model put the notes 4 to 47 cents sharp. So each hole keeps its scaled position and its diameter is solved, bottom hole first, until its note lands on equal temperament against A4 = 440Hz:

Hole Note Scaled from the original Solved Change
1 C5 8.27mm 8.10mm −0.17mm, 2% smaller
2 D5 8.42mm 7.35mm −1.07mm, 13% smaller
3 E5 8.34mm 7.26mm −1.08mm, 13% smaller
4 G5 8.34mm 7.76mm −0.58mm, 7% smaller
5 A5 5.86mm 6.35mm +0.49mm, 8% larger

Holes 2 and 3 depart most from the original pattern. In it they were the size of holes 1 and 4, and now they are the two smallest of the four large holes. Every note lands within 0.01 cents of equal temperament, and the fundamental, with every hole closed, sits 0.8 cents above 440Hz.

The builds at the original size were removed at the author's asking; the original's own files are still in yours/original/. The tweaks made in redrawing it:

  1. Millimetre-true, in the house cut-order colours, kerf-compensated, with every part numbered. The originals are drawn in Inkscape's 1/90in units with no kerf.
  2. The plug is four 3mm discs, 12mm instead of 11. The exit hole moves 1mm toward the mouth to make room.
  3. In the fingers build the top is finger-jointed to the sides, like the bottom, so it press-fits instead of being glued. Its joint is the trumpet bores' one, 6mm fingers with no play, instead of the original's 10mm fingers.
  4. The rings are the original's own image, each kept in the original's proportion to its hole, so they grow and shrink with the re-solved holes. The ramp is left out, because the engraved bevel did not work.
  5. With the edge filed by hand, the top is one piece, as in every build: file the edge before assembly.

Beyond the scaling, nothing acoustic is changed. The mouthpiece cap and the block's layers are glued, as they were. The leather tie is its own file: cut it from leather, not ply.
